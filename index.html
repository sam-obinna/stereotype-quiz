Why the gut gets it wrong

Representativeness

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed in the 1970s that people judge probability by asking “how much does this person look like my picture of a librarian?” instead of “how many librarians are there?” Similarity feels like evidence. It usually isn’t enough.

Base-rate neglect

The size of each group (the base rate) is the most important number in every one of the first-type questions, and the description makes you forget it. A trait that fits 1 in 20 farmers still points to a farmer when farmers outnumber librarians seven to one.

The conjunction fallacy

In Tversky and Kahneman’s 1983 study, about 85% of participants ranked “feminist bank teller” as more likely than “bank teller” for Linda, which is impossible. Adding a detail that fits the story makes it feel more plausible, while making it strictly less probable.
